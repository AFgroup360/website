Growth

Growth does not fail on margin. It fails on timing.

Every new order is funded by you before the customer funds it. Win enough of them quickly enough and the bank balance goes backwards.

There is a version of trouble that looks exactly like success. Revenue up, order book full, team busy, and the account emptier every month. Owners in that position often assume they have a pricing problem. Usually they have a timing problem.

What actually happens

Take a straightforward order. You buy materials and pay a deposit. You run payroll while the work is done. You invoice on completion. The customer pays on their terms, which are rarely your terms.

From the day your money left to the day theirs arrived, you financed that order. The wider that window, the more cash each order consumes. Nothing here is a mistake. It is arithmetic.

Now double the order book. The margin per job has not changed, so the profit line looks better than ever. But you are now financing twice as many of those windows at the same time, and the cash required has doubled with it. That is working capital absorbing growth, and it is the most common reason a growing business ends up asking a lender for money in a hurry.

Growth is not free. It is funded, and until somebody finances it, it is funded by you.

The three levers

The window has three sides and every one of them is negotiable.

  • Get paid sooner. Invoice the day the work is done rather than at month end. Chase on a schedule rather than when it becomes urgent. Take deposits on work that warrants them.
  • Hold less. Inventory and work in progress are cash sitting still. The right number is the smallest one that does not cost you a sale.
  • Pay in step. Supplier terms that match when your customers pay, rather than terms nobody ever renegotiated. This is often the fastest win, and it is the one owners raise last.

We have closed that gap for a family business by realigning supplier terms so money going out matched money coming in. The interest bill came down with it. No new financing was required.

Why it has to be monthly

These numbers move constantly. A quarterly look tells you what the gap was. A monthly rhythm, with receivables and payables watched by age and a forecast rebuilt on what actually happened, tells you what it is about to be. That is the difference between managing the growth and being surprised by it.
